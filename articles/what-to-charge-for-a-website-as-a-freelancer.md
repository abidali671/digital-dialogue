CONTENT:

Charge for a website by the thing you are building and the days it takes you, not by whatever number a stranger typed into a job post. A landing page, a business site, and a small web app are three different products. They should not share one price, and they should not share one proposal.

The figures below are a way to think, not a global rate card. Your speed, your country's costs, and Upwork's fee all move the final number. Look up the fee in your account and subtract it before you decide a price is "fine."

## Start from days, then add the fee

Write how long the last similar site actually took, including revisions and the boring deploy. If you have never timed yourself, time a demo this week. Guessing "a weekend" for a five-page site is how weekends become three weeks.

Pick the amount you want to keep per day after the freelancer fee. Multiply by the days. That total is the client-facing price only after you gross it up for the fee. If you need $700 net and the fee is 10 percent, you bill about $778, not $700. If you skip that step, every project is silently discounted.

Round to a number you can say out loud. Then attach it to a written scope. A price without a page list is a discount waiting to happen.

## A landing page

A landing page is one screen with a clear action: a form, a booking link, a checkout for a single offer. It can include your layout, their words, mobile layout, and one round of revisions. It does not include a blog, a logo, or five extra sections they invent after seeing the draft.

For many freelancers this is one to three days. Price the days, not the fact that it is "just one page." A page with custom interactions, a messy brand, or copy you are expected to write is a longer job. Say so and raise the number, or cut those pieces out.

On Upwork, this is the right size for a first fixed-price contract if the client already has text and photos. It is the wrong place to prove you can build their whole company.

## A business site

A business site is a small set of pages: home, about, services, contact, maybe a simple gallery. Same rules: they supply the words unless copy is in the price. Two revision rounds is enough to write into the contract. A third round is a new milestone.

This is often a one to two week job for a developer who is not also the brand designer. If you are designing from a blank page, add days or decline the design half. Developers who "include branding for free" are not being generous. They are hiding unpaid days inside a site price that was already tight.

A sensible price is several times the landing page, because the work is several times the pages plus a shared header, footer, and the time you lose to content that arrives late. If their budget matches your landing page number, sell the landing page. Do not compress five pages into that budget and call it a package.

## A small web app

A small web app has accounts, saved data, or a workflow: a client portal, a booking tool with a database, an internal dashboard. It is not a business site with a contact form. The unknowns are larger, so a single fixed price for "the app" is how you end up maintaining it for months.

Price a first slice instead. Example: sign-in, one main screen, and a way to edit one kind of record, in a set number of weeks, with a weekly check. Later slices are new milestones. If the client cannot describe the first slice, you are not late on a quote. They are early on a spec. Offer a paid short discovery milestone, or walk away.

Hourly can fit this slice if the client accepts a weekly cap. Fixed price can fit if the slice is truly fixed. Do not mix "unlimited tweaks" with a fixed app price. That phrase is the scope leak.

## How to say the number on Upwork

In the proposal, tie the price to the list:

- Landing page, their copy, one revision round, delivered in a week, for X
- Or five pages, your existing logo, two revision rounds, delivered in two weeks, for Y

Put X or Y where they can see it. Add that extra pages are a separate milestone. If their posted budget is lower, answer with the smaller product, not with an apology and a match of their budget.

Raise the price when they add copywriting, a shop, or a rush date. Do not raise it halfway through because you feel busy. The time to change the number is before you accept the offer.

## A floor you will actually keep

Write your three floors in a note: landing page, business site, first slice of a small app. Each floor is the client-facing price after the fee math. When a job pays under the floor, you do not "take it for the review" unless you shrink the scope until it sits on the floor again.

Clients can tell when a price is random. They can also tell when you know what a page costs. The second version gets fewer jobs and better ones, which is the point of having a rate at all.
